# Why this skill is designed the way it is

The research behind every rule in `SKILL.md`. Read this when tempted to deviate.

---

## 1. The core failure: fluency illusion

Rereading raises *processing ease* — the material gets smoother to read each pass. The metacognitive system misreads smoothness as memory strength. So the learner stops studying a topic precisely when it is still fragile, and feels confident while doing it.

> Roediger & Karpicke (2006): one group reread a passage 4×; another read it once and self-tested 3×. A week later the testing group recalled **~50% more** — despite spending *less* total time with the material.

**Design consequence:** she is never allowed to open notes before attempting recall. The blank page is not a quiz — it's a fluency-illusion breaker. It shows her, in her own handwriting, the gap between "feels known" and "is known."

**Why learners resist:** weak strategies feel successful *in the moment*. Strong strategies feel like failing. She must be told this explicitly, once, or she'll interpret difficulty as evidence the method is wrong.

---

## 2. Retrieval practice (the testing effect)

Meta-analyses put retrieval practice at **30–50% better long-term retention** than passive review. Retrieval isn't measurement of memory — it *modifies* memory, strengthening the path back to the item.

**Design consequence:** every session is structured as retrieval-first. Cold open, blank page, teach-back, gates. Reading is a support activity, not the main one.

---

## 3. Spacing

Cepeda et al. (2006): optimal gap between reviews ≈ **10–20% of how long you need to remember it**. For a placement season ~6 months out, that's gaps measured in weeks, expanding. Spaced learners retain 2–3× more than crammers at equal total hours.

Classroom trials of 1-3-7-14-28 day flashcard schedules show large post-test gains over controls.

**Design consequence:** the 0 / +1 / +3 / +7 / +16 / +35 ladder, and `ledger.md` as a real dated schedule rather than a suggestion. Left informal, spacing silently degrades into cramming.

---

## 4. Successive relearning = retrieval + spacing, to criterion

The strongest known combination: retrieve *until correct* in a session, then repeat that across spaced sessions. Not "review until it looks familiar" — retrieve to a **criterion**.

**Design consequence:** the 🟢 criterion is *fast, cold, out loud*, and a concept must hit 🟢 **twice consecutively** before leaving the schedule. One good day is noise.

---

## 5. Cognitive load — the reason we don't just pile on difficulty

Working memory holds only a few elements. CS material is **high element-interactivity** (a graph algorithm requires holding traversal + data structure + invariant + complexity simultaneously) — intrinsic load is already near ceiling.

Critical finding: **desirable difficulties stop being desirable when working memory is already stressed.** Adding challenge to high-interactivity material for a struggling learner produces *undesirable* difficulty — it degrades learning rather than deepening it.

**Design consequence:**
- Max 3 new concepts/session; overflow defers.
- Chunking is mandatory; 200-word cards; one screen per day.
- Blocked practice **first**, interleaving only after the concept is stable.
- Scaffolding fades across gates rather than being absent early.

This is the rule most likely to be broken by generic curriculum instincts. Don't break it.

---

## 6. Interleaving — powerful, but sequenced

Interleaving builds the ability to *identify which pattern applies* — which is the actual coding-interview skill, since no interviewer says "this is a sliding window problem." But interleaving is harder than blocked practice and **struggling learners need help with it**.

Standard resolution: stabilize the concept with massed practice, *then* mix it in.

**Design consequence:** interleaving lives in weekly consolidation and later gates — with problems **unlabelled** so she must classify before solving — never in the first exposure.

---

## 7. Elaboration, dual coding, concrete examples

These three are amplifiers — they work best **as part of retrieval practice**, not as standalone study activities. Reading a diagram is not dual coding; reproducing it from memory is.

Dual coding also has a failure mode: decorative or redundant visuals *increase* extraneous load (multimedia overload).

**Design consequence:** every card has one diagram that carries information the text doesn't — and the blank page requires her to *redraw* it, not admire it. Anchors are the elaboration hook: a single contrast or analogy that links new material to something she already owns.

---

## 8. Metacognition — the missing layer

Students routinely can't see their own gaps, and get so absorbed in *doing* that they never ask why. Self-testing is what makes gaps visible. But learners need the **how, when, and why** taught explicitly — handing someone flashcards doesn't produce retrieval practice; most people flip through them passively.

**Design consequence:**
- She self-marks ✅/⚠️/❌ after every cold open — calibration training.
- Root-cause tags in the Mistake Book turn vague failure into a diagnosis.
- Monthly: report her dominant tag back to her. `careless` needs a checklist, not more hours. `concept-gap` needs re-teaching. Different failures, different fixes.

---

## 9. Effort ≠ progress (the framing that matters most for her)

Her effort is already maximal. It is not a lever — it's a constant. The only lever is **where the effort lands**.

Time spent on re-exposure is not merely less efficient; it's actively misleading, because it manufactures confidence. She has been paying full price for study and receiving partial delivery, and the confidence it produced hid the shortfall.

**Design consequence:** never praise hours. Praise retrieval attempts, surfaced errors, and 🔴→🟢 transitions. Never say "study more" — she already does. Say "study this, from this prompt, on this date."
