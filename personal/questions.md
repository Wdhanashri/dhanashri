# The 25 questions — with your answers

**~90 min to build. Then 5 minutes before every interview.**

> These are known in advance. There is nothing to *learn* — only to decide what you think and rehearse saying it. That makes this the highest-return hour in your whole preparation.

---

## ⓪ Pretest — 3 min. **Write a guess.**

1. They ask why your CGPA is 7.58. What's your first sentence?
2. They ask about a time you failed. Which story do you reach for?

## The five stories

**Almost every behavioural question is answered by one of five stories.** Prepare these once and reuse them everywhere — that's the trick nobody tells you.

Use **STAR**: Situation → Task → Action → **Result**. *Most people forget the Result. It's the part being scored.*

| # | Story | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The QR fix at preCICE | problem-solving · unfamiliar codebase · initiative |
| **2** | Maintainer review feedback | receiving criticism · iterating · humility |
| **3** | A hard CartWise integration | technical challenge · persistence · debugging |
| **4** | MIC Master / hackathon | teamwork · pressure · deadlines |
| **5** | MCUT Taiwan selection | initiative · going after something |

> ✍️ **Write all five now, four lines each, in STAR form.** Do this before reading the questions below — the questions get much easier once the stories exist.
>
> **1.** ________________________________________________
> **2.** ________________________________________________
> **3.** ________________________________________________
> **4.** ________________________________________________
> **5.** ________________________________________________

### What STAR actually sounds like

The difference is not the content — it's the shape. Same story, both ways:

```
❌ "The QR codes were broken so I fixed them by using a different
    library and it got merged."

    → no situation, no reasoning, no result. 8 seconds, nothing learned.

✅ S  "The preCICE docs site had QR codes on printable pages so you
       could scan a printout and reach the live version."
   T  "They'd silently stopped rendering and nobody had picked it up."
   A  "I traced it to Google deprecating their Chart API, and replaced
       it with a client-side library so there's no external dependency
       at all. I added print-only CSS so it shows on paper, not screen."
   R  "It merged after a round of review, and the print navigation
       works again. It also taught me not to depend on a third-party
       service for something you can do locally."

    → 40 seconds. Four parts. The Result carries a lesson.
```

**The Result is the part that gets scored**, and it's the part everyone drops. If your answer ends at "and then I fixed it", you've told them nothing.

**Fresher-level expectation, honestly:** they want to hear that you did something real, thought about it, and can describe it calmly. **Nobody expects a leadership epic.** Four sentences per story is the right size — under a minute, out loud.

# The Big 5 — word-perfect

### Q1 · "Tell me about yourself"
→ `intro.md`. **60 seconds. Rehearsed.**

### Q2 · "Why should we hire you?"
> *"Three reasons. I've shipped a full-stack system end to end, so I'm not only comfortable with one layer. I've worked in a codebase I didn't write and got changes through someone else's review, which is closer to what the job actually is. And I finish things — I'd rather complete one thing properly than start several."*

**Three specifics, no adjectives.**

### Q3 · "What is your greatest weakness?"
**The rule: a real weakness + what you're actively doing about it.** Not a disguised strength.

> *"I used to move on from a topic as soon as I understood it, and then find months later that I'd lost it. I've changed how I study because of that — I now test myself on old material on a schedule instead of re-reading it. It's made a big difference to how much actually sticks."*

**This is true, it's specific, and it shows self-correction.** Interviewers can tell the difference between this and "I'm a perfectionist."

### Q4 · "Where do you see yourself in five years?"
> *"Still building — but with more ownership. Right now I've built things alone or with one reviewer; in five years I'd want to be the person others come to on a system, and to have gone deep on one area rather than staying broad."*

**Don't say "in a managerial position."** For an SDE role it reads as wanting to leave engineering.

### Q5 · "Do you have any questions for us?"
**Never say no.** Have three ready; ask two.

```
"What does the first six months look like for someone joining as a fresher?"
"How is work usually reviewed here — is there a code review culture?"
"What's something a new joiner typically finds harder than they expected?"
```

**That third one is excellent** — it gets an honest answer, and it signals you're thinking about actually doing the job.

# The tricky 6 — prepare these properly

### Q6 · "Why is your CGPA 7.58?"
**One honest sentence. No apology. Redirect once. Then stop.**

> *"It's not the number I'd have liked. I put a lot of my time into building things — the project and the open-source work — and my grades reflect that trade-off more than my ability. What I'd point to is that the work I did outside coursework is the work I can actually defend in detail."*

**Then stop talking.** Do not keep explaining — over-explaining is what makes it sound like a problem.

```
❌ blaming teachers, marking, the syllabus, health, family
❌ five minutes of justification
✅ one sentence, one redirect, silence
```

### Q7 · "Tell me about a time you failed"
**Use story 2 or 3.** The result is what matters — *what changed because of it*.

> *"When I first opened a PR on preCICE, I ___________. The maintainer pointed out ___________. I'd assumed ___________, and I hadn't ___________. I reworked it and it merged. Since then I read a lot more of the existing code before I change anything."*

*(Fill this in from your actual PR threads.)*

**The trap:** picking a "failure" that isn't one. *"I failed to finish early"* reads as a dodge, and they'll ask again.

### Q8 · "Tell me about a conflict in a team"
**Use story 4.** Aim for a *disagreement*, not a *falling-out* — and show you heard the other side.

> ________________________________________________

**Never criticise a teammate as a person.** Disagree with an approach, not with someone's character.

### Q9 · "Do you have other offers?"
- **If no:** *"Not currently — I'm interviewing with a few companies."* Honest, and not weak.
- **If yes:** say so plainly without using it as leverage.
- **Never invent one.**

### Q10 · "What are your salary expectations?"
As a fresher, almost always:
> *"I'm expecting whatever is standard for the role — I'm more focused on the work and what I'd learn early on."*

Only give a number if pushed twice, and then a range you've checked for that company and city.

### Q11 · "Are you willing to relocate?"
Decide **now**, in advance. Answer in one word plus one clause.
> *"Yes — I'm open to relocating."*

Hesitating here reads as unprepared even when the answer is yes.

# The standard 14 — one or two lines each

### Q12 · "Why did you choose CS with AI?"
> *"I wanted the core CS — DSA, OS, DBMS, networks — and AI on top rather than instead. CartWise is roughly that shape: the LLM does the interesting part, but most of the work was the backend around it."*

### Q13 · "Which language are you most comfortable with?"
**Decide this once and say it everywhere.**
> *"Python. It's what I use for DSA and for the CartWise backend. I've also built in Java and JavaScript."*

> ⚠️ **Your resume lists Java first.** If you answer "Python", reorder the resume — or you'll get handed a Java question. **Fix this before you apply.**

### Q14 · "Rate yourself out of 10 in ___"
**Never say 9 or 10.** 7 or 8, with a boundary.
> *"About a 7 in Python. I'm comfortable with the language and its data structures; I haven't done much with concurrency or performance tuning."*

**Naming the boundary is what makes the number credible.**

### Q15 · "What do you know about our company?"
Two specifics, researched the night before. **One product/team + one recent thing.** Ten minutes on their site is enough, and most candidates don't do it.

### Q16 · "How do you handle pressure or deadlines?"
Use story 4. Describe **what you actually do** — a method, not a feeling.
> *"I cut scope before I cut quality. In the hackathon we ___________."*

### Q17 · "Are you a team player or do you prefer working alone?"
> *"Both, honestly. CartWise I built mostly alone; the open-source work only happened because I was working with maintainers. I'd say I can do either, but I learn faster with review."*

### Q18 · "What if you're asked to work on a tech stack you don't know?"
> *"That's most of what I've done. I hadn't used Jekyll before preCICE. I'd read the existing code first, make the smallest change that works, and check it with someone who knows it."*

**That's a real answer with real evidence** — much better than "I'm a fast learner."

### Q19 · "What are your hobbies?"
Answer honestly and briefly. **Not** "coding" — that's the job. Languages (you're learning Japanese) is a genuinely good one, and it's on your resume.

### Q20 · "Tell me something not on your resume"
Have one prepared. Something human, 20 seconds, true.

### Q21 · "What if you don't get placed?"
> *"I'd keep building and keep applying off-campus. The preparation doesn't stop being useful."*

Calm, not desperate.

### Q22 · "Short-term and long-term goals?"
Short: get good at production-grade engineering in the first year. Long: depth in one area. **Same shape as Q4.**

### Q23 · "Why should we prefer you over someone with a higher CGPA?"
**Don't get defensive.**
> *"I'd say look at what we can each explain. I can walk through every decision in my project and every change I made in an open-source repo, including the ones I got wrong."*

### Q24 · "You're from Vadodara — any problem with our location?"
Combine with Q11. One word, then move on.

### Q25 · "Anything you'd like to add?"
Have one line — usually the thing you most wish they'd asked about.
> *"Only that the open-source work is the part I'd most like to talk about, if there's time."*

## The four rules for all of it

```
1. STOP TALKING when you've answered. Silence is the interviewer's job to fill.
   Rambling is what turns a good answer into a bad one.

2. EVERY claim gets evidence. "I'm a fast learner" → "I hadn't used Jekyll before."

3. NEVER criticise anyone — a college, a teammate, a previous team.
   It always reflects on you, never on them.

4. "I don't know" + how you'd find out beats a confident guess. Every time.
```

## Drill — 30 min. Record it.

```
Round 1   the Big 5, out loud, from memory
Round 2   the Tricky 6 — especially Q6 (CGPA). Say it, then STOP. Time the silence.
Round 3   shuffle all 25. Answer 10 at random, cold, no preparation between them.
Round 4   listen back to Round 3. Note every ramble and every unevidenced claim.
```

> ✍️ **Blank page, 5 min.** Write the five stories in one line each. Then your CGPA answer, word for word.
